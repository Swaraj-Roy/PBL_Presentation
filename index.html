Problem Statement

Urban Heat Islands (UHIs) are localized regions where urban temperatures significantly exceed surrounding rural areas — a direct consequence of heat-absorbing built surfaces, reduced vegetation cover, and anthropogenic heat release from vehicles, industry, and buildings.

The effects are not uniform. A single city can contain neighborhoods differing by up to 10°C at the same time, yet conventional weather forecasting operates at resolutions of 25–50 km, completely missing these life-critical micro-scale variations.

With climate change intensifying heatwave frequency and magnitude, the need for hyperlocal, actionable, near-term temperature forecasts — at the neighborhood (≤100 m) and hourly scale — has never been more urgent.

🌡️
Public Health Risk
Heat-related mortality and morbidity surge during unforecasted extreme heat events. Vulnerable populations — elderly, outdoor workers, low-income households — bear disproportionate risk. 24–48h advance warning enables life-saving interventions.
⚡
Energy Grid Stress
Cooling demand spikes driven by urban heat islands create unpredictable peak loads. Hyperlocal forecasts enable demand-side pre-cooling and load balancing, reducing grid strain.
🏙️
Urban Planning Blindspot
City planners currently lack fine-grained thermal maps to guide green infrastructure placement, reflective pavement deployment, and cool corridor design.
⚖️
Climate Equity
Historically underserved neighborhoods often have lower tree cover and higher impervious surface fractions, amplifying UHI effects precisely where adaptive capacity is weakest.
01.1 —

Literature Review

FOUNDATIONAL
UHI Phenomenology
Oke (1982) established the energetic basis of UHIs — surface energy balance disruption caused by altered albedo, thermal mass, and reduced evapotranspiration. Voogt & Oke (2003) extended this to thermal remote sensing. Imhoff et al. (2010) quantified UHI intensity across USA biomes using MODIS data.
REMOTE SENSING
Satellite LST Products
Landsat 8/9 thermal bands (~100m, 16-day revisit) offer high spatial resolution. MODIS MOD11A1 provides daily 1km LST. Wan (2014) validated Collection-6 MODIS LST products. The core trade-off: Landsat for spatial detail vs. MODIS for temporal density — motivating data fusion approaches.
STREET IMAGERY
Visual Urban Features
Klimenko et al. (2023) demonstrated that infrared urban surface temperatures can be estimated from street-view imagery. Zhang et al. (2018) showed that Green View Index (GVI) from street scenes correlates with 0.5–1.5°C temperature reductions. Pretrained CNN embeddings provide compact micro-scale context.
IOT NETWORKS
Dense Sensor Deployments
Barthwal & Sharma (2022) demonstrated mobile IoT-based UHI detection. Hölken et al. (2025) deployed semantic-web-enabled IoT networks. Low-cost sensors (DHT22, SHT31) at ±0.3–0.5°C accuracy enable dense deployments ($5–15 per node) previously unaffordable with professional weather stations (~$700).
DEEP LEARNING
Spatio-Temporal Models
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997) introduced LSTM for temporal sequences. Ronneberger et al. (2015) pioneered U-Net CNN encoders. Vaswani et al. (2017) introduced Transformers. Combining CNN spatial encoders with LSTM temporal modules represents a well-validated, implementable approach for environmental forecasting.
HEALTH IMPACT
Heat Mortality Studies
Gasparrini et al. (2015, The Lancet) found heat-attributable mortality accounts for 0.5–1.5% of deaths in warm regions. Eisenman et al. (2016) linked built environment characteristics directly to heat-related mortality. Climate Central (2024) identified 65 cities with critical urban heat hotspots.

🔬 Research Gap & Our Innovation

Existing systems are either too coarse (ERA5 at 25km cannot resolve neighborhood-scale variation), too slow (Landsat revisits every 16 days), or too expensive (dense professional sensor networks cost $700+ per node). No existing deployed system simultaneously fuses satellite LST, street-level visual features, and IoT sensor readings into a single lightweight model for hyperlocal 24–48h forecasting at 100m resolution with practical cost constraints.

Our contribution: A practical, reproducible CNN+LSTM pipeline that fuses all three modalities, deployable on a single GPU with ~$20,000 annual operating cost for a 100km² city pilot — making hyperlocal heat forecasting accessible to municipal governments in developing economies.

🔭
Spatial Encoder (CNN)
A 3–4 layer CNN ingests a 7×7 neighborhood patch of LST + static channels around each grid cell. ReLU activations and batch normalization extract localized spatial features. Street-image PCA embeddings (64-d, reduced from ResNet-50's 2048-d) and static covariates are concatenated to the flattened CNN output to form a rich spatial representation.
⏱️
Temporal Model (LSTM)
A two-layer LSTM processes 24-hour sequences of fused spatial embeddings plus lagged sensor readings (t−24 to t). Hidden dimension is kept at 256 units to maintain practical compute requirements. Teacher-forcing during training stabilizes multi-step prediction. Validation uses held-out whole-day blocks to prevent temporal data leakage.
⚙️
Training Configuration
AdamW Optimizer LR: 1×10⁻⁴ Batch: 32 Max 80 Epochs Early Stop: 10
Data split: 70% training · 15% validation · 15% test — all in continuous temporal blocks to simulate real deployment conditions. Augmentation: spatial flips for CNN patches + time dropout for robustness.
02.3 —

Preprocessing & Multi-Modal Fusion

🗺️
Spatial Harmonization
All sources reprojected to common UTM zone. Satellite rasters resampled bilinearly to 100m×100m. IoT point values spatially interpolated via inverse-distance weighting within 500m radius.
🕐
Temporal Harmonization
Sensor readings aggregated to hourly averages. MODIS daily values assigned to nearest hour. Landsat scenes used as spatial baselines with conservative linear interpolation between passes.
🖼️
Street Image Features
ResNet-50 (ImageNet weights) truncated at global pooling layer. 2048-d feature vectors reduced via PCA to 64-d. Green View Index (GVI) and Sky View Factor (SVF) proxies computed via semantic segmentation heuristics.
✅
Quality Control
Sensor time series screened for outliers (Z-score > 4) and drift patterns. 3 Davis Vantage Pro2 reference stations used to periodically recalibrate low-cost sensor subsets via linear regression correction.

Deployment & System Architecture

📥
Data Ingestion Layer
Four parallel ingestion streams: Satellite LST (Landsat/MODIS via GEE), Street Imagery (GSV/Mapillary API), IoT Sensors via MQTT/LoRaWAN gateways, and Meteorological reanalysis (ERA5/HRRR). All streams land in AWS S3 / GCS object storage for raw data persistence.
MQTTLoRaWANGEE APIAWS S3
🔄
ETL + Feature Store
AWS Lambda / Cloud Functions handle serverless ETL. Processed data flows to three stores: PostGIS (spatial rasters/vectors), TimescaleDB (time-series sensor data), and Redis (hot feature cache for inference). Scheduled hourly runs keep feature store current.
PostGISTimescaleDBRedis Cache
🧠
ML Training & Registry
Model training on SageMaker / Vertex AI GPU cluster (single NVIDIA T4/V100 sufficient for pilot). MLflow / W&B model registry tracks experiments and versions. Automated retraining triggered when data drift is detected.
SageMakerMLflowNVIDIA T4
🚀
Inference & Alerting
FastAPI inference service provides REST/GraphQL endpoints for 1h/6h/24h/48h forecasts. Mapbox/Plotly dashboard renders live heat maps. Alerting system triggers SMS/email when forecasts exceed threshold temperatures for vulnerability-weighted neighborhoods.

🎯 Use Cases & Societal Impact

Urban Planning — Hyperlocal forecasts guide placement of green infrastructure, cool pavements, reflective roofing. Scenario modelling quantifies cooling benefits (e.g., 20% albedo increase → 0.8°C reduction).
Public Health — 24–48h advance warnings enable cooling center activation, vulnerable population outreach, and heat emergency resource pre-positioning.
Energy Management — Short-term forecasts support demand-side pre-cooling schedules, reducing peak electricity loads and grid instability during heatwaves.
Climate Equity — Prioritize sensor placement in historically underserved neighborhoods; aggregate outputs to neighborhood-level before public release to protect privacy.
04.1 —

Limitations & Future Work

⚠ Current Limitations
🔋
Sensor Drift
Low-cost sensors require periodic re-calibration against reference stations. Drift of ±0.3–0.8°C observed over 3-month deployment windows.
☁️
Landsat Cloud Cover
16-day revisit with cloud contamination reduces spatial LST availability in monsoon months. MODIS fills gaps but at 10× coarser resolution.
🌍
City Transferability
Models trained on one city require transfer learning for different climate zones, urban morphologies, and seasonal patterns.
🌊
Complex Microclimates
Waterfronts, dense tree canopies, and steep topography create sub-100m thermal variability that 100m grid cannot fully resolve.
🔮 Future Work
🌬️
Heat Stress Indices
Integrate humidity and wind more tightly to model apparent temperature, WBGT, and physiologically meaningful heat stress indices.
📊
Probabilistic Forecasting
Quantile regression and ensemble methods to produce calibrated uncertainty bands, enabling risk-aware decision making for health authorities.
🔄
Cross-City Transfer Learning
Semi-supervised domain adaptation to transfer models across cities with minimal local sensor data — enabling rapid deployment in data-scarce regions.
📱
Citizen Science
Smartphone-based opportunistic sensing (GPS + on-device temperature) for hyper-dense observations, particularly in areas with limited IoT deployment budgets.
